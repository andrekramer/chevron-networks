From Chevron Nets & IDL to Chevron Attention
Paired channels and inertial difference learning as choice, selection and retained change in a Transformer.



How does a learning system change without losing the structure that allows it to remain coherent?

That may be the central question behind Chevron Networks.

If you have been following along this series of posts you can skip straight to Section 5: Chevron Attention. That’s the new idea this post introduces.

Modern neural networks are extremely effective at adapting parameters to reduce error. But adaptation alone is not enough for a continuously learning system. A system must also decide which changes are temporary, which are noise, which are context-specific, and which should become part of what it retains.

Chevron Networks begin with a small architectural change. Instead of representing each unit with a single activation, they give it two coupled channels. Instead of connecting units with scalar weights, they use small (2 times 2) operators.

Inertial Difference Learning, or IDL, then adds a learning principle:

Learning is not merely the reduction of error. It is the controlled retention of difference under inertia.

The two ideas belong together. The Chevron supplies an internal distinction. IDL determines how that distinction develops through time. Both fast and slow.

But the unrestricted form creates an immediate problem.

The interesting question is not whether two channels are more expressive than one. They plainly are.

The interesting question is:

What happens when the two channels are required to remain different kinds of thing?

3. From Free Chevron to Structured Chevron
Chevron Networks can be divided into three broad regimes.

Free Chevron
The full (2 times 2) operator is unconstrained. The network discovers whatever use it can make of the two channels.

This is the broadest and most expressive form, but it is also the least distinctive. Without additional constraints, reviewers can reasonably ask whether it is merely another parameterization of an ordinary real-valued network.

Structured Chevron
A Structured Chevron assigns the channels different functions and enforces those functions through architecture, learning rate, input routing, plasticity, or loss.

Examples include:

proposal and constraint;

content and control;

evidence and expectation;

policy and value;

memory and reset;

content and doubt;

current model and retained model.

The emerging lesson from our first experiments is that this structured regime is the most promising.

The Chevron becomes distinctive not when it contains two numbers, but when the two numbers are prevented from becoming interchangeable.

5. What the early experiments suggest
The current results remain small-scale and preliminary.

Free Chevron models have not shown broad superiority on ordinary tasks. In simple classification experiments they generally matched, rather than exceeded, conventional baselines. They also learned the same shortcuts when the training data allowed degenerate solutions.

That negative result is important. The Chevron shape alone does not create reasoning, robustness, or meaningful opposition.

More interesting results appeared when the channels were given explicit roles.

In a contextual-veto experiment, a base capability was learned through one set of paths, while a later contextual override was learned through separate cross-channel paths. Freezing the base structure allowed the model to acquire the veto without forgetting the original rule.

This did not prove that Chevron Networks automatically solve catastrophic forgetting. The preservation was produced by deliberate architectural separation. But that is itself informative: the (2\times2) form provides a natural interface for separating a retained core from a contextual modulation path.

Later IDL experiments tested temporary contradiction against persistent change.

The first experiment showed that a persistence-sensitive gate behaved in the predicted direction. Retention remained lower during short contradictory bursts and rose strongly after a genuine regime shift.

A second experiment showed that the IDL model moved its retained channel less during a temporary disturbance than an always-updating slow model, while still opening retention during a later sustained change.

These results do not yet establish better overall learning performance. A conventional model may adapt faster immediately after a shift.

The more modest finding is:

(A), (N), and persistent (A-N) difference can be made experimentally visible, and the difference can regulate when slow structure changes.

That is enough to justify a continuing research programme. Especially if the alternative is to IDLy stand by.

10. Adaptive Resonance Theory
Adaptive Resonance Theory, or ART, is an important historical neighbour.

ART addresses the stability–plasticity dilemma: how can a system remain capable of rapid learning without allowing new inputs to overwrite previously learned categories?

Its broad pattern is:

bottom-up evidence activates a candidate category;

a top-down expectation is compared with the input;

sufficient match produces resonance;

excessive mismatch triggers reset and search;

learning occurs when a stable interpretation is found.

6. Hierarchical Chevron Attention
Different layers or scales may assign different meanings to (A) and (N).

For example:

lower levels: sensory evidence / prediction;

middle levels: semantic proposal / category template;

upper levels: action policy / retained norm or self-model.

Higher retained channels may constrain lower adaptive channels, while persistent lower-level mismatch may revise higher retained structure.

This produces a dynamic hierarchy rather than a fixed sequence of identical blocks.

The human brain may be understood as an evolved hierarchy in which each layer was shaped by different evolutionary pressures, building new forms of adaptation on top of older retained structures rather than replacing them outright.

Chevron Networks should not be described as literal models of the brain.

Transformers do not implement biological query–key softmax attention, and ordinary backpropagation is not a direct model of synaptic learning.

Biological systems repeatedly separate:

fast and slow learning;

excitation and inhibition;

representation and gating;

current sensory evidence and retained expectation;

action proposal and action suppression;

15. The research programme
The progression is now coherent:

Chevron Networks introduce paired states and (2 times 2) cross-coupling.

Structured Chevrons assign non-interchangeable roles to the channels.

IDL makes (A) fast, (N) slow, and persistent (A - N) difference the regulator of retention.

Chevron Predictive Architecture applies the distinction to changing predictive regimes.

Chevron Attention applies it to retrieval, veto, abstention, and reopening.

ART-like resonance and reset may add search and category formation.

Allostatic replay may allow unresolved differences to be revisited offline rather than immediately written into retained structure.

These are not unrelated architectural experiments. They are increasingly concrete forms of one hypothesis:

A continuously learning system should preserve an internal difference between present adaptation and retained organization, and use the persistence and consequences of that difference to regulate what becomes part of itself.

The Chevron provides the geometry.

IDL provides the temporal rule.

Transformer Attention provides a new site where the distinction can be tested.

The difficult task remains empirical: demonstrating that this structure produces useful learning behaviour that cannot be explained merely by additional capacity, ordinary gating, or carefully engineered task-specific separation.

But the idea is now sufficiently precise to be falsifiable.

Can (A) adapt quickly?

Can (N) preserve slower structure?

Can (A - N) distinguish temporary disturbance from persistent change?

Can retrieved content be vetoed without being erased?

Can repeated mismatch reopen search or revise retained organization?

Can the system remain plastic without collapsing its continuity?

Those are small experimental questions.

Together, they address a much larger one:

How does a system remain itself while changing?
