## Tension-Guided Replay: Learning What Needs To Be Rehearsed

  The next allostatic chevron experiment tested a more Batesonian question.

  The earlier experiments showed that replay helps. A chevron net with replay forgot much less than the same model without replay, and it used replay more efficiently than a scalar MLP
  baseline.

  But that replay was still externally imposed. The system did not decide what mattered. It simply replayed stored examples.

  So the next question was:

  > Can the model’s own A/N tension tell it what it needs to rehearse?

  In other words, can replay become internally guided?

  This matters because the allostatic story is not just about memory. It is about regulation. A system should not only remember past experience. It should learn which experiences remain
  unresolved, which need consolidation, and which should be brought back into training.

  That is the first step from replay as storage toward replay as something more like dream.

  ## From Uniform Replay To Tension-Guided Replay

  The chevron model has two channels:

  A = fast adaptive channel
  N = slower retained or normative channel

  The key internal signal is A/N disagreement:

  D = mean(|A - N|)

  This measures how far the fast adaptive channel has drifted from the slower retained channel.

  The hypothesis was simple:

  > The model should replay what it has not reconciled.

  So I compared three replay policies:

  uniform
  disagreement
  loss_disagreement

  Uniform replay samples stored examples evenly.

  Disagreement replay prioritizes examples with high A/N tension.

  Loss + disagreement replay combines classifier loss with A/N disagreement.

  The important distinction is this:

  loss says: replay what the classifier currently finds hard
  A/N disagreement says: replay what the system has not reconciled

  Those are not the same signal.

  A high-loss example may be noisy, ambiguous, or simply difficult under the current classifier. A high-tension example indicates a mismatch between fast adaptation and slower retained
  structure. In the allostatic framing, that second signal is closer to the thing we care about.

  ## First Replay Policy Sweep

  The focused test used:

  model: full chevron
  readout: A-only
  buffer sizes: 100 and 500
  seeds: 1 through 5

  A-only readout matters because it prevents the N channel from directly feeding the classifier. N can only help by shaping A through the chevron coupling.

  The first sweep gave:

  | Replay Policy | Buffer | Forgetting | Final Avg Acc | Old-task Avg | Current-task Acc |
  |---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
  | uniform | 100 | 0.3795 | 0.6864 | 0.6123 | 0.9828 |
  | disagreement | 100 | 0.3760 | 0.6863 | 0.6141 | 0.9753 |
  | loss_disagreement | 100 | 0.3853 | 0.6816 | 0.6051 | 0.9875 |
  | uniform | 500 | 0.2092 | 0.7909 | 0.7695 | 0.8768 |
  | disagreement | 500 | 0.1983 | 0.7949 | 0.7749 | 0.8749 |
  | loss_disagreement | 500 | 0.2394 | 0.7332 | 0.7250 | 0.7660 |

  The result was modest but informative.

  Pure disagreement replay slightly improved forgetting over uniform replay, especially at buffer 500:

  uniform buffer 500:      forgetting = 0.2092
  disagreement buffer 500: forgetting = 0.1983

  That is not a dramatic improvement. But it is in the right direction.

  The more interesting result was negative: adding loss made things worse.

  At buffer 500, loss_disagreement increased forgetting and reduced both final average accuracy and current-task accuracy. That suggested that raw loss may be a bad replay signal in this
  setup.

  ## Is Loss The Wrong Signal?

  This raised a useful question.

  Maybe loss is not merely a weaker signal. Maybe loss points at the wrong kind of thing.

  Loss measures classifier failure. But allostatic replay is not only about correcting errors. It is about reconciling fast adaptation with retained structure.

  A high-loss example might be:

  - noisy
  - ambiguous
  - recently displaced by task switching
  - hard for superficial classifier reasons
  - unrepresentative of the deeper stability-plasticity conflict

  A high A/N disagreement example is different. It says:

  > The fast channel and slow channel are not aligned here.

  That is closer to the allostatic object of interest.

  In Batesonian terms, the system should not merely rehearse what it got wrong. It should rehearse what remains unresolved at the level of context.

  Uniform replay is memory.

  Loss replay is correction.

  Tension-guided replay is closer to learning what still needs integration.

  ## Tuning Loss

  To make sure this was not just a scaling problem, I changed the priority formula.

  Instead of combining raw loss and raw disagreement, I normalized each signal separately:

  priority = disagreement_weight * normalized_disagreement
           + loss_weight * normalized_loss

  Then I swept loss weights at buffer 500:

  loss_weight = 0.05
  loss_weight = 0.10
  loss_weight = 0.25
  loss_weight = 0.50

  The results:

  | Policy | Forgetting | Final Avg Acc | Old-task Avg | Current-task Acc |
  |---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
  | uniform | 0.2092 | 0.7909 | 0.7695 | 0.8768 |
  | disagreement | 0.1983 | 0.7949 | 0.7749 | 0.8749 |
  | loss weight 0.05 | 0.2067 | 0.7848 | 0.7660 | 0.8601 |
  | loss weight 0.10 | 0.1995 | 0.7860 | 0.7720 | 0.8419 |
  | loss weight 0.25 | 0.2189 | 0.7805 | 0.7565 | 0.8767 |
  | loss weight 0.50 | 0.2135 | 0.7720 | 0.7599 | 0.8205 |

  This confirmed the pattern.

  A small amount of loss, especially 0.10, nearly matched pure disagreement on forgetting. But it did not beat it, and it lowered current-task accuracy.

  Larger loss weights degraded performance.

  So the conclusion is not that loss is useless. It may still help in a better-tuned or different task setting. But in this first allostatic replay experiment, loss was not the right primary
  signal.

  A/N disagreement was cleaner.

  ## The Batesonian Reading

  This is where the experiment becomes more interesting than the numbers.

  Bateson’s “learning to learn” is not just about improving a task. It is about changing the conditions under which future learning happens.

  Uniform replay does not do that. The experimenter decides what is rehearsed.

  Tension-guided replay starts to shift that decision inside the system.

  The system is no longer only learning task labels. It is beginning to use its own internal mismatch to decide what deserves further learning.

  That is a primitive version of:

  learning what needs to be learned again

  Or:

  learning what remains unresolved

  The key move is from external curriculum to internal salience.

  The model is not yet forming new contexts. It is not yet splitting regimes. It is not yet doing full Batesonian Learning II. But it is taking a step in that direction.

  It is using the relation between fast adaptation and slow retention to shape its own future training distribution.

  That is why the small improvement from disagreement replay matters. Not because the number is large. It is not. But because the direction is right.

  ## Current Interpretation

  The result so far is:

  > A/N disagreement is mildly useful as a replay priority signal.

  More importantly:

  > Loss appears to be a worse primary replay signal than internal A/N tension.

  This supports the allostatic interpretation.

  The point of the N channel is not merely to store old answers. It provides a slower reference against which current adaptation can be measured. When A and N disagree, the system has found
  something that may need rehearsal.

  That is different from ordinary error.

  Error says:

  the answer is wrong

  Tension says:

  the system has not reconciled this experience with what it has retained

  Those are related, but not identical.

  For an allostatic learner, the second may be the deeper signal.

  ## What Comes Next

  The next experiments should make this sharper.

  First, test more replay formulas:

  priority = D
  priority = D^2
  priority = rank(D)
  priority = softmax(kD)
  priority = D + small_loss

  Second, check whether disagreement predicts future forgetting:

  Does high A/N tension after task k predict which examples or tasks degrade after task k+1?

  Third, separate transient tension from persistent tension.

  A momentary spike may mean surprise. Persistent tension may mean unresolved context.

  That distinction matters because the larger chevron hypothesis is not only replay.

  It is:

  low tension -> update
  medium tension -> replay
  high persistent tension -> split

  The present experiment only tested the middle step.

  But that is the right next step. Before a system can split contexts, it should first show that its internal tension can identify what needs to be replayed.

  So the practical result is modest.

  The conceptual result is sharper:

  > Uniform replay remembers the past. Tension-guided replay begins to ask which parts of the past have not yet been understood.
